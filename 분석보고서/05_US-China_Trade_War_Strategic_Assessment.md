  </h1>
  <div style="font-size: 14px; color: #475569; font-style: italic; margin-bottom: 22px; line-height: 1.4;">

As of September 2026, both countries remain under the one-year truce reached at the November 2025 Busan summit, though disputed outcomes from the May 2026 Beijing summit and a reported US review of new 'overcapacity' tariffs in August 2026 point to renewed friction.
  </div>

  <!-- 4. Section 1: BACKGROUND AND SCOPE -->
  <h2 style="font-size: 15px; font-weight: 900; color: #0F172A; border-bottom: 1.5px solid #CBD5E1; padding-bottom: 4px; margin: 26px 0 10px 0; letter-spacing: 0.5px; text-transform: uppercase;">
    1. BACKGROUND AND SCOPE
  </h2>
  <p style="font-size: 13px; color: #334155; text-align: justify; margin-bottom: 12px;">
    The US-China trade war, dating to the first Trump term's 2018 tariffs, was reignited under Trump's second term (Jan 2025-) through IEEPA 'fentanyl' tariffs, the 'Liberation Day' reciprocal tariffs, product-specific hikes from the Section 301 four-year review, and Chinese rare-earth export controls. After a 2025 roller-coaster year that briefly pushed tariffs above 100%, the October-November 2025 Busan and Washington agreements provisionally contained the conflict, though the structural contest over semiconductors, rare earths, and advanced technology remains unresolved.
  </p>
  <p style="font-size: 13px; color: #334155; text-align: justify; margin-bottom: 14px;">
    <strong>Impact of the Trump Administration:</strong> President Trump has cycled through multiple legal authorities -- IEEPA, Section 301, Section 232 -- to run tariff policy as an ongoing negotiating tool, and after the Supreme Court invalidated the IEEPA tariffs in February 2026, his administration quickly reconstituted equivalent tariffs under alternate authorities. China tariffs have also doubled as leverage in bilateral deals with allies including Taiwan, Korea, and the EU.
  </p>

Notably, both the 2025 and 2026 summits largely sidestepped the core disputes over semiconductors and Taiwan.

  <!-- 6. Section 3: ECONOMIC AND SECURITY IMPACT ANALYSIS -->
  <h2 style="font-size: 15px; font-weight: 900; color: #0F172A; border-bottom: 1.5px solid #CBD5E1; padding-bottom: 4px; margin: 26px 0 10px 0; letter-spacing: 0.5px; text-transform: uppercase;">
    3. ECONOMIC AND SECURITY IMPACT ANALYSIS
  </h2>
  
  <div style="margin-bottom: 14px;">
    <div style="font-size: 13px; font-weight: 700; color: #1E293B; margin-bottom: 4px;">■ Security & Defense Dimension</div>
    <ul style="font-size: 13px; color: #334155; margin: 4px 0 8px 20px; padding: 0;">
      <li style="margin-bottom: 4px;"><strong>North Korea & Regional Security:</strong> The US-China trade war is not a directly linked issue to North Korea, and this research found no direct evidentiary link to the nuclear or missile programs. Intensifying US-China strategic competition could, at most, function as an indirect backdrop raising overall great-power tension around the Korean Peninsula.</li>
      <li style="margin-bottom: 4px;"><strong>Alliance Impact:</strong> The Korea-US alliance was effectively redefined through this tariff negotiation as a security-and-trade 'package deal' -- tariff relief exchanged for large-scale investment and shipbuilding cooperation -- illustrating how the alliance's scope is expanding from military security into industrial and supply-chain cooperation.</li>
      <li style="margin-bottom: 4px;"><strong>Military & Missile Threats:</strong> This issue is fundamentally an economic-security matter centered on tariffs and export controls, with no direct connection established to North Korean missile threats. If anything, it illustrates the broader trend of treating strategic-goods export controls (semiconductors, rare earths) as an 'economic security threat' alongside, rather than in place of, traditional military-security threats.</li>
    </ul>
  </div>

  <div style="margin-bottom: 14px;">
    <div style="font-size: 13px; font-weight: 700; color: #1E293B; margin-bottom: 4px;">■ Economic, Trade & Financial Dimension</div>
    <ul style="font-size: 13px; color: #334155; margin: 4px 0 8px 20px; padding: 0;">
      <li style="margin-bottom: 4px;"><strong>Trade & Tariffs:</strong> Direct US-China trade has contracted under high tariffs, but overall trade flows have persisted through rerouting via third countries and supply-chain restructuring -- the same dynamic behind EU complaints about rising Chinese trade diversion through Europe. Semiconductor (HS 8541/8542) trade data for Jan 2025-Jun 2026 (USITC DataWeb) show US exports to China at roughly $17.05bn versus imports of about $2.59bn -- exports outweighing imports more than sixfold -- suggesting the US continues selling substantial semiconductor volumes to China even as it restricts imports of advanced logic chips. The figures aggregate advanced and legacy products alike, so they speak to overall trade scale rather than to the effectiveness of export controls themselves.</li>
      <li style="margin-bottom: 4px;"><strong>Foreign Exchange & Currency:</strong> Korea's decision to cap its US investment package at $20 billion annually and structure it through a risk-sharing special-purpose vehicle reflects an explicit effort to manage the potential shock of large-scale outbound investment on the won's exchange rate.</li>
      <li style="margin-bottom: 4px;"><strong>Cross-Border Investment:</strong> In advanced sectors like semiconductors, tariff and export-control uncertainty is incentivizing onshoring of US production capacity, while Korean firms are recalibrating investment strategy under the dual pressure of expanded US investment commitments and continued uncertainty in their China operations.</li>
    </ul>
  </div>

  <div style="margin-bottom: 20px;">
    <div style="font-size: 13px; font-weight: 700; color: #1E293B; margin-bottom: 4px;">■ Public Opinion & Expert Consensus</div>
    <p style="font-size: 13px; color: #334155; margin: 0 0 4px 0;"><strong>Public Sentiment:</strong> Domestic opinion is divided -- concerns persist over the fiscal and currency risks of the $350 billion US investment commitment, even as securing semiconductor treatment on par with Taiwan is seen as a genuine negotiating win.</p>
    <p style="font-size: 13px; color: #334155; margin: 0;"><strong>Expert Assessment:</strong> Most trade experts characterize the 2025-2026 US-China agreements not as a strategic resolution but as a tactical truce driven by each side's domestic political and economic needs, expecting the underlying technology-hegemony contest over semiconductors and rare earths to persist structurally for the foreseeable future.</p>
  </div>

  <!-- 7. Section 4: RISK OUTLOOK AND UNCERTAINTIES -->
  <h2 style="font-size: 15px; font-weight: 900; color: #0F172A; border-bottom: 1.5px solid #CBD5E1; padding-bottom: 4px; margin: 26px 0 10px 0; letter-spacing: 0.5px; text-transform: uppercase;">
    4. PERFORMANCE BENCHMARKS AND GAP ANALYSIS
  </h2>
  <div style="font-size: 13px; color: #334155; margin-bottom: 12px;">
    <p style="margin: 0 0 6px 0;"><strong>Short Term (3-6 months):</strong> Over the coming months, conditions are likely to remain broadly stable within the framework of the November 2025 deal (tariff exclusions extended through December 31, 2026; general licenses for rare earths in place), even as sporadic pressure tactics -- like the 'overcapacity' tariff floated in August 2026 -- continue to surface.</p>
    <p style="margin: 0 0 8px 0; color: #1E40AF;"><em>▶ Most Likely Scenario:</em> The most probable near-term scenario is continued 'managed tension' -- periodic sector-specific measures (chip equipment, shipbuilding, select materials) rather than a full-scale re-escalation, alongside ongoing negotiation.</p>
    <p style="margin: 0 0 6px 0;"><strong>Medium Term (1-3 years):</strong> Renegotiation is expected around the November 2026 expiration of the Busan truce, with tariff levels either easing further or ratcheting back up depending on the outcome.</p>
    <p style="margin: 0 0 8px 0; color: #1E40AF;"><em>▶ Expected Inflection Point:</em> The expiration of the truce around November 2026, and any further Trump-Xi summit preceding it, are likely to be the key inflection points shaping the next phase of the conflict.</p>
    <p style="margin: 0 0 6px 0;"><strong>Long Term Structural Trajectory:</strong> Most trade experts expect a structural 'managed decoupling' to take hold in strategic industries -- semiconductors, rare earths, batteries -- settling into neither full integration nor complete severance but a friend-shoring/near-shoring-based reordering of supply chains.</p>
    <p style="margin: 0 0 12px 0; color: #1E40AF;"><em>▶ Structural Changes:</em> Beyond tariff rates, the conflict is reshaping the structure of global trade policy itself -- institutionalizing export controls on strategic goods (chip equipment, advanced AI chips, rare earths) and normalizing bilateral investment-for-tariff-relief deals as a policy tool.</p>
  </div>

  <div style="background-color: #F1F5F9; border-left: 3px solid #64748B; padding: 10px 14px; margin-bottom: 24px; font-size: 12px; color: #334155;">
    <strong>Key Geopolitical Uncertainties:</strong>
    <ol style="margin: 4px 0 0 18px; padding: 0;">
      <li style="margin-bottom: 2px;">Whether the one-year Busan truce (due to expire around November 2026) will be renewed, or whether tariff-escalation threats resurface as the deadline approaches, is the single largest near-term variable.</li>
      <li style="margin-bottom: 2px;">Even after the administration rebuilt tariffs under Section 232/301/122 authorities following the Supreme Court's February 2026 IEEPA ruling, further litigation or congressional efforts to constrain executive trade authority could destabilize the tariff regime itself.</li>
      <li>An escalation of Taiwan-related tensions could rapidly expand controls and retaliation across the entire semiconductor supply chain, reflecting the structural entanglement of trade negotiations with security issues.</li>
    </ol>
